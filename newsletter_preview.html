News Article 1: A 1.9 billion-year-old bedrock will soon house the world’s first permanent nuclear waste site
Finland is preparing to open Onkalo, the world's first permanent underground repository for spent nuclear fuel, excavated into 1.9-billion-year-old bedrock on the island of Olkiluoto. Posiva, the company managing the project, plans to seal radioactive rods in copper canisters, bury them over 400 meters deep, and surround them with bentonite clay, expecting radioactivity to decline over hundreds of thousands of years. Proponents, including Posiva, view this as the "missing point" for sustainable nuclear energy, arguing it offers superior safety compared to vulnerable surface storage and reflects Finland's commitment to self-managing its nuclear waste. However, Edwin Lyman of the Union of Concerned Scientists highlights "uncertainties," particularly concerning the eventual corrosion of copper canisters and the long-term risks primarily borne by future generations. To address this, nuclear semiotics is exploring methods like ceramic plates with "nuclear messages" to warn distant future societies about the buried waste.

Hans Jonas, author of *The Imperative of Responsibility*, would view Finland's nuclear waste site as a profound manifestation of humanity's unprecedented ethical challenge in the age of advanced technology. Jonas would argue that this project embodies an *asymmetry* of power and responsibility: present generations gain the benefits of nuclear energy, while future, unknowable generations bear the potentially catastrophic, irreversible risks without consent. He would question the hubris in believing that current scientific methods can definitively predict the integrity of copper canisters and bentonite clay over "hundreds of thousands of years," given the inherent limits of human knowledge and the dynamism of geological processes. The concept of "nuclear semiotics" – ceramic plates with warnings – would strike Jonas as a poignant but ultimately inadequate gesture. For him, true responsibility is not merely about warning future peoples of a burden we imposed, but about refraining from actions whose consequences we cannot genuinely guarantee to be compatible with the permanence of human life on Earth. He would insist that the "heuristic of fear" demands we anticipate the worst-case scenario with such long-term, irreversible risks, rather than relying on optimistic projections, highlighting that the "uncertainties" are not mere technical hurdles, but fundamental moral quandaries.

News Article 2: Ecuador hikes tariffs to 100-percent in feud with neighbour Colombia
Ecuadorian President Daniel Noboa's government has escalated trade tensions with Colombia, imposing a 100% tariff on Colombian imports, effective May 1. This move follows previous tariffs of 30% and 50% and is justified by Ecuador's Ministry of Production, which accuses Colombia of failing to implement "concrete and effective measures" against drug trafficking and to enhance border security. Ecuador asserts that security and the fight against drug trafficking are "non-negotiable priorities." In response, Colombian President Gustavo Petro stated that Ecuador's actions signify "the end of the Andean Pact" for Colombia and called for a shift in trade focus towards Mercosur and other regional partners. The dispute reflects broader political rifts, with Noboa, a right-wing leader, echoing criticisms from figures like Donald Trump regarding Petro's (left-wing) drug enforcement efforts, despite Colombia's significant drug seizures and its "Total Peace" policy.

Thucydides, the historian of the Peloponnesian War, would immediately look beyond Ecuador's stated justifications of drug trafficking and border security to uncover the deeper "true causes" of this escalation. Much like the disputes between Greek city-states, he would suspect a confluence of fear, honor, and interest. Is Ecuador's aggressive stance truly driven by a profound security threat, or is it a calculated move to assert national honor, reflecting President Noboa's perceived need to demonstrate strength and decisive leadership, perhaps for domestic political advantage? Thucydides would identify the "non-negotiable priorities" as a classic rhetorical maneuver used to mask underlying economic protectionism or a strategic bid for regional leverage. The unilateral imposition of tariffs and Colombia's declaration of "the end of the Andean Pact" perfectly illustrate the fragility of alliances and cooperative agreements when national interests, or perceptions thereof, diverge. This echoes the breakdown of inter-polis alliances under stress. Thucydides would caution that such actions, driven by a perception of strength or a desire to impose one's will, rarely lead to lasting stability and often risk a wider cycle of retaliation, much like the escalating tit-for-tat actions that spiraled into the Peloponnesian War, transforming a regional dispute into a broader geopolitical realignment.

News Article 3: Heading home: The riskiest part of the Artemis II moon mission is still ahead
The Artemis II mission's astronauts face their most perilous challenge during reentry into Earth's atmosphere, a phase marked by extreme speeds and temperatures exceeding 5,000 degrees Fahrenheit. This concern is heightened by a known issue with the Orion capsule's heat shield, which, during the uncrewed Artemis I flight, returned with concerning pockmarks and cracking. A compromised heat shield could lead to catastrophic failure without an escape mechanism for the crew. While NASA acknowledges the heat shield's "less than ideal" nature, mission managers express confidence in safely returning astronauts Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen. They assert that changes to the reentry strategy, based on extensive analysis, sufficiently address the heat shield's limitations, though the high stakes of this critical final phase are recognized.

Max Weber, examining the Artemis II mission, would identify this scenario as a compelling illustration of the limits and paradoxes within modern rational-legal bureaucracies, such as NASA. The agency's confidence, despite a "less than ideal" heat shield, stems from an intensive process of "extensive analysis" and "changes to the reentry strategy"—hallmarks of technical rationality and bureaucratic problem-solving. Weber would see this as an attempt to master uncertainty through calculability and expertise, a defining characteristic of disenchanted modernity. However, he would also point to the inherent irrationality embedded within this rational framework: the calculated gamble to proceed with known flaws, balancing the imperative to advance the mission (driven by national prestige, scientific goals, and funding cycles) against the irreducible risk to human life. This tension reveals the "iron cage" of rationality, where the drive for systematic control can sometimes lead to a blind spot regarding truly novel or unpredictable events. The managers' "confidence" becomes not just a technical assessment, but also a performative act within the bureaucratic structure, aiming to reinforce the legitimacy and competence of the institution even in the face of profound, and potentially catastrophic, uncertainty.

Commentary: Why this ceasefire may have made Iran stronger [Channel News Asia (CNA)]
Bamo Nouri and Inderjeet Parmar argue that despite the recent US-Israel-Iran conflict and the assassination of senior Iranian leaders, the ensuing ceasefire may have paradoxically strengthened Iran. They contend that Iran's regime demonstrated institutional resilience by rapidly replacing assassinated officials and maintaining cohesion, indicating it was not militarily defeated. The ceasefire, they suggest, was negotiated around Iranian conditions, delivering strategic gains previously unavailable to Tehran, with Iran's 10-point plan serving as the framework. This plan includes demands for sanctions relief, access to frozen assets, reconstruction support, and continued oversight of the Strait of Hormuz. Reopening the Strait under Iranian control, along with proposed transit fees, could provide crucial revenue for rebuilding and expanding regional influence, illustrating how a military campaign failing to achieve decisive political outcomes can, unexpectedly, create new opportunities for the targeted state.

Carl von Clausewitz would undoubtedly concur with the article's central thesis, viewing the outcome through his lens that "war is merely the continuation of policy by other means." He would assert that the military actions by the US and Israel, including assassinations, ultimately failed because they did not achieve their political objectives of weakening or destabilizing the Iranian regime. Instead, the resulting ceasefire, negotiated on Iran's terms and delivering substantial strategic gains—sanctions relief, access to assets, and control over the Strait of Hormuz—demonstrates a profound disconnect between military means and political ends. Clausewitz would emphasize that Iran's "institutional resilience" and cohesion represent critical moral factors in warfare, illustrating that a state's will and internal strength can defy purely material assessments of power. The ability to rapidly replace leaders and maintain political coherence under pressure is a testament to an enduring political purpose. This scenario perfectly exemplifies Clausewitz's concept of "friction" and the "fog of war," where unintended consequences and the adversary's adaptability can completely alter the strategic landscape, turning what might have been intended as a punitive campaign into an unexpected opportunity for the targeted state to achieve its political aims through the very cessation of hostilities.

War crimes are no longer shameful. That should terrify you [Al Jazeera]
Philippe Bolopion argues that leaders in the United States, Israel, and Iran are exhibiting a "swaggering contempt" for international laws protecting civilians, a dangerous shift from past attempts to mask or deny war crimes. He cites US President Trump's disregard for international law and Defense Secretary Hegseth's call for "maximum lethality" and "no quarter" as examples of this overt dismissal. Bolopion further points to Israeli Defence Minister Katz's threats to demolish homes in southern Lebanon and Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps declaring civilian banks and commercial ships as legitimate targets. The author highlights the devastating human cost, including thousands killed and millions displaced, and a US attack on an Iranian elementary school. Bolopion warns that by rejecting the principle of nonreciprocity, leaders risk a world without rules where brute force prevails, ultimately endangering their own populations through escalating retaliatory actions.

Thomas Hobbes, observing the "swaggering contempt" for international law described by Bolopion, would grimly recognize humanity's regression toward the "state of nature." Hobbes argued that without a common power to enforce rules, life becomes "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," a "war of every man against every man." The explicit dismissal of norms like "no quarter" and the targeting of civilians, he would contend, are clear signs that the tenuous social contract of international relations is dissolving. The "terrifying" aspect, from a Hobbesian perspective, is that this overt disregard for established rules removes any predictable limits on conflict, fostering a universal climate of fear and insecurity. While Hobbes focused on the need for a sovereign within a state, his logic extends to the international arena: when powerful states or actors reject the principle of nonreciprocity, they dismantle the very framework of mutual self-interest that underpins international law. This erosion means that no state, including those initiating such actions, can ultimately be secure, as they are inviting reciprocal brutality. Ultimately, for Hobbes, the pursuit of "absolute liberty" to wage war without restraint leads not to strength, but to a collective vulnerability that endangers all populations, including their own.
